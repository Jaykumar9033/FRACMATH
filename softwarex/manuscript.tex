\title{FRACMATH: A vectorized MATLAB framework for continuum damage
mechanics with crack band regularization}

\author[unm]{Jaykumar~Mavani}
\author[unm]{Madura~Pathirage\corref{cor1}}
\ead{mpathirage@unm.edu}
\cortext[cor1]{Corresponding author}
\address[unm]{Gerald May Department of Civil, Construction, and Environmental Engineering,
University of New Mexico, Albuquerque, NM, USA}

\begin{abstract}
FRACMATH is an inspectable, vectorized MATLAB implementation of isotropic
damage with direction-dependent crack-band regularization. Its code links
projected width, constitutive history, sparse assembly and solution.
Material-point tests verify strain mapping, irreversible damage and
fracture-energy calibration. Mesh/increment studies compare regularized
and fixed softening laws. Ninety observations of thirty matched computing configurations assess
MATLAB CPU, hybrid GPU and Abaqus SMP configurations. The hybrid GPU shows
no speed advantage on the tested workstation. A geometry-matched
three-dimensional pure-tension comparison supplies experimental error
measures; mixed-mode and torsion images illustrate workflows. Source,
histories, diagnostics and reproduction scripts accompany the paper.
\end{abstract}

continuum damage mechanics \sep crack-band regularization \sep MATLAB \sep hybrid GPU \sep Abaqus UMAT \sep quasibrittle fracture
\end{keyword}

\section{Motivation and significance}

\texttt{FRACMATH} is intended for researchers and students who need to
inspect the numerical path from a strain field to a damage field and a
global response. The constitutive law and crack-band principle are established
methods \citep{deVree,oliver1989,bazant_oh}. The code shows the assumptions, update order, regularization length
and numerical limits so that users can check and change them. The package therefore includes element-level routines, a 2D bending
case, two 3D examples, plotting scripts, and an Abaqus UMAT for independent
comparison. The controlled study examines how element-size compensation
affects structural response and dissipation, and how increment size and
sparse linear algebra limit the numerical workflow.

\subsection{Related software and scope}

General-purpose finite element systems such as Abaqus \citep{abaqus} and
OOFEM \citep{oofem} provide broader element and analysis capabilities.
CALFEM \citep{calfem} provides an accessible MATLAB finite element teaching
environment. \texttt{FRACMATH} offers a narrower, code-readable example of
direction-dependent local crack-band damage in 2D and 3D. It is designed
for teaching, replication, and testing method changes.

\section{Software description}

\subsection{Software architecture}

The package follows a visible sequence: input settings and mesh files;
precomputed element operators; displacement increments and constitutive
history; sparse equilibrium solution; saved numerical states; and figure
reconstruction. The 2D entry \texttt{start\_here.m} exposes material,
increment and backend settings and calls the benchmark solver. Mesh inputs
contain coordinates, connectivity and support/loading sets. Outputs contain
load--CMOD histories, damage snapshots, residuals and component timings.
The beginner guide describes units and array dimensions; the implementation
walkthrough maps each output to its producing function. Separate 3D drivers
retain their documented equilibrium algorithms. The optional UMAT and hybrid
GPU paths share the 2D constitutive definitions while using different
execution and equilibrium procedures.

\subsection{Software functionalities}

A scalar damage variable $\omega\in[0,1]$ scales the elastic stress:
$\boldsymbol\sigma=(1-\omega)\mathbb C_0:\boldsymbol\varepsilon$.
The history variable $\kappa_{n+1}=\max(\kappa_n,\tilde\varepsilon_{n+1})$
retains maximum equivalent strain. Committing
$\omega_{n+1}=\max(\omega_n,\omega_{\rm trial})$ enforces damage
irreversibility when the projected width changes. The modified von Mises measure is
\begin{equation}
\tilde\varepsilon = \frac{k-1}{2k(1-2\nu)}I_1
  + \frac{1}{2k}\sqrt{\left(\frac{k-1}{1-2\nu}\right)^2I_1^2
  + \frac{12k}{(1+\nu)^2}J_2},\quad
J_2=\frac{(e_1-e_2)^2+(e_2-e_3)^2+(e_3-e_1)^2}{6},
\label{eq:equiv}
\end{equation}
where $k=f_c/f_t$. For the 2D plane-stress element,
$e_3=-\nu(e_1+e_2)/(1-\nu)$. Both the MATLAB and UMAT implementations use Eq.~\eqref{eq:equiv}.

The damage law is
\begin{equation}
\omega(\kappa)=1-\frac{\kappa_0}{\kappa}
\exp\!\left[-\frac{\kappa-\kappa_0}{\varepsilon_f-\kappa_0}\right],
\quad \kappa_0=f_t/E,\quad
\varepsilon_f=\frac{\kappa_0}{2}+\frac{G_F}{h f_t}.
\label{eq:softening}
\end{equation}
The Oliver projected width for a three-node triangle is
\begin{equation}
h(\mathbf n)=\frac{2}{\sum_{a=1}^3|\nabla N_a\cdot\mathbf n|},
\label{eq:oliver-t3}
\end{equation}
with $\mathbf n$ along maximum principal strain. The 3D tetrahedral
implementation uses the analogous projected width. Its exponential law uses
$\beta=f_t h/G_F$ and $\omega=1-(\kappa_0/\kappa)
\exp[-\beta(\kappa-\kappa_0)]$, capped at 0.999999. Here $G_F$
calibrates post-peak work; Eq.~\eqref{eq:softening} calibrates total
uniaxial work in 2D. These are local
regularization formulas; the material-point energy check below does not
by itself prove structural mesh objectivity.
The pure-tension 3D implementation retains both $\kappa$ and $\omega$;
accepted increments commit their nondecreasing histories, while rejected
trials leave the committed state unchanged.

\subsection{Numerical implementation}

The 2D driver \texttt{solver\_main\_3pb.m} reads the mesh and support/loading
sets, precomputes elastic element matrices and shape-function gradients,
and advances prescribed displacement. At each increment it (1) assembles
the secant stiffness from the previous damage field, (2) factors its free
block with \texttt{decomposition}, (3) solves equilibrium for the frozen
damage state, (4) obtains element strains with \texttt{pagemtimes}, updates
$h$, $\kappa$, and $\omega$ in \texttt{damage\_update}, and (5) reassembles
the damaged stiffness for reaction forces and the next increment. The
factorization helper first attempts Cholesky and falls back to LU if
that attempt fails. It is reused only within a displacement increment. There is no
outer equilibrium iteration after the damage update, so this is a
sequential secant scheme rather than a fully converged Newton solve.
The post-update free-DOF residual is exported for each increment.

For example, the projected-width calculation acts on each triangle's
three gradient pairs. With \texttt{gradN}, \texttt{nx} and \texttt{ny}
denoting those gradients and principal-direction components, its MATLAB form is
\begin{verbatim}
projected = gradN(:,1:2:end).*nx + gradN(:,2:2:end).*ny;
h = max(2./max(sum(abs(projected),2),1e-14),1e-12);
\end{verbatim}
This width enters Eq.~\eqref{eq:softening}; \texttt{kappa} and
\texttt{omega} retain the irreversible element histories.

The optional Abaqus path uses \texttt{run\_3pb\_abaqus\_OLIVER\_T3\_FAST.py}
to write a table of T3 shape-function gradients. The Fortran
\texttt{cdm\_umat\_2d\_OLIVER\_T3\_FAST.for} reads that table to compute
Eq.~\eqref{eq:oliver-t3}; it does not use \texttt{CELENT} when the table
is present. The UMAT supplies the degraded elastic secant matrix as
\texttt{DDSDDE}, rather than a consistent derivative of the evolving
damage law. Its approximate tangent requires reporting equilibrium
iteration counts with timings. \texttt{UEXTERNALDB} initializes the
gradient table before SMP workers start; material calls read its saved
arrays, and missing gradients terminate the job.

The optional 2D hybrid GPU backend keeps strain operators and reference
element matrices on the device. A fused \texttt{gpuArray.arrayfun} kernel
evaluates projected width, equivalent strain, and irreversible history.
Results and stiffness values return to CPU sparse assembly and factorization.
Timed operations include transfers and synchronization.

\subsection{Verification and reproducibility}

OpenAI Codex assisted with implementation, audit and plotting scripts as
well as manuscript preparation. Verification uses executed MATLAB/Fortran
routines, archived solver outputs and explicitly identified experimental
digitization. Figures are deterministic plots of these data. The beginner
entry reproduces benchmark numerical arrays exactly; rebuilding the eight
generated figure assets gives identical pixels, and the two supplied geometry
illustrations retain their source bytes. These checks establish reproduction
of the archived outputs; illustrative 3D fields retain the validation limits
reported below.


One-element tests verify tensile/compressive equivalent-strain mapping,
irreversible unloading/reloading and $h\int\sigma\,d\varepsilon\approx G_F$
for four projected widths (Table~\ref{tab:energy}). A driver linked to the
unchanged UMAT reproduces the MATLAB energy values. Sixteen additional
width/direction combinations pass, with maximum relative energy error
$1.78\times10^{-6}$. These local checks do not establish structural
orientation independence.
An additional 712-case audit compares the actual UMAT with an independent
tensor-eigensolve calculation and the MATLAB damage functions. Strain splits,
multiaxial states, irreversible history, projected width, stress and the
documented secant matrix pass declared absolute/relative tolerances of
$2\times10^{-10}$. This does not establish a consistent damage tangent.

\subsubsection*{Size, mesh and computing configurations}

Thirty configurations, each measured three times, combine depths of 100/200~mm, three meshes, and MATLAB
CPU1/CPU8/hybrid GPU or Abaqus CPU1/CPU8. All dimensions scale by two;
final displacement is $-0.1/-0.2$~mm. Absolute global/notch seeds are
12.5/2.5, 6.25/1.25 and 4.6875/0.9375~mm; each computing comparison
uses identical mesh files. The largest mesh has 100{,}104 elements and
101{,}088 DOFs. MATLAB uses 2{,}000 fixed increments; Abaqus uses maximum
increments of $1/2{,}000$, with SDV every 100 accepted increments.
The two finest large-specimen Abaqus meshes use limits of 80 equilibrium
iterations and 12 attempts, retaining default convergence tolerances;
a failed default-control attempt is archived. Fresh processes run
sequentially; the third observation reverses the computing-configuration order.
MATLAB CPU8 limits numerical-library
threads; the hybrid backend uses an NVIDIA RTX A2000 12GB in double precision.

All hardware-response checks pass: MATLAB load-history differences are
below $1.76\times10^{-11}$ of peak load and final-damage differences below
$1.34\times10^{-9}$; Abaqus pairs have identical curves at CSV precision
and identical iteration counts. MATLAB CPU8 and hybrid GPU are slower
than CPU1 in all six cases. CPU1 factorization occupies 67--76\% of loop time.
Abaqus SMP speed ratios range from 0.68 to 2.90; the largest mesh reaches
2.90. Abaqus CPU1 sparse-solver passes occupy 9.5--14.4\% of analysis wall
time; the remainder is unallocated. MATLAB/Abaqus peak differences are
1.87--4.24\%. Table~\ref{tab:scaling} and Figure~\ref{fig:timing} report
median costs and observed ranges from 90 runs; complete phase, memory and
residual records accompany the code. All saved MATLAB numerical arrays
and Abaqus response CSVs are identical across repeats within each setting.

Two separate small/coarse, single-CPU runs measure UMAT calls and profile
native assembly work. Both complete 2{,}000 increments and reproduce all
2{,}001 baseline response rows and seven mesh/boundary hashes exactly.
An instrumented UMAT records 18{,}579{,}456 calls with a raw elapsed sum
of 2.996~s. Integer-8 \texttt{SYSTEM\_CLOCK} reads surround the unchanged
constitutive calculations. Empty clock pairs average 0.09~$\mu$s;
their call-count projection is 1.672~s. This diagnostic is not subtracted:
clock overhead, timer resolution and scheduling qualify the raw sum.
The instrumented job reports 3{,}184 solver passes, 3{,}105 decompositions,
no cutbacks, 15.33~s of sparse-solver elapsed time and 162~s analysis wall time.

A separate VTune collection attaches only to \texttt{standard.exe} after
startup. It estimates 1.917~s of user-library self work and 2.873~s in four
named assembly routines. All report exports succeed. Temporary-library
removal, missing symbols and PulseEvent warnings limit attribution.
The profile totals 139.31~s of sampled CPU estimates, while Abaqus reports

\subsection{Benchmark 2: 3D Nooru-Mohamed Tests}
% =====================================================================

The Nooru-Mohamed specimen is a square double edge notched concrete
panel, $200 \times 200 \times 50$~mm. The illustrative mesh uses two
20~mm-deep, 5~mm-wide horizontal notches from opposite edges; the published
specimen has 25~mm-deep notches \citep{nooru1992}. The
central ligament between the notch tips experiences a mixed
mode stress state. The illustrative proportional-displacement case prescribes a vertical (mode I)
displacement on the top edge and a proportional horizontal
(shear) displacement on the side edges, with a shear to tension ratio
$\gamma = 0.6$ and the bottom edge held fixed, ramped together under
displacement control over 900 increments to a top displacement of
0.5~mm. The material is concrete with $E = 29{,}000$~MPa, $\nu = 0.20$,
$f_t = 3.0$~MPa, $G_F = 0.110$~N/mm, and $k = f_c/f_t = 10$ (so
$f_c = 30$~MPa).

The proportional-loading damage panels illustrate the workflow. The saved states do not all meet the equilibrium tolerance, so these
panels are used only as a qualitative example. The option name
\texttt{4c} selects proportional displacement control, not the published
sequential experimental loading. A separate
pure-tension comparison uses specimen 47-05 with two glued platens
(Figure 3.16, printed page 45 of \citep{nooru1992}). Its load--displacement
curve is manually digitized; estimated reading uncertainty is
$\pm 0.00102$~mm and $\pm 164$~N. This quantitative case uses the published
25~mm-deep, 5~mm-wide notches, with 35,917, 94,525 and 144,791 TET4 elements
(21,828, 54,540 and 84,774 DOFs). Global/notch mesh seeds are 12/4, 9/3
and 6/2~mm. Material parameters above are retained
without fitting. Bottom vertical displacements are fixed, the top platen
has a common unknown vertical displacement, and minimal lateral pins
remove rigid modes. The mean displacement of four interpolated 65~mm
vertical gauges at $x=30/170$~mm on both faces controls the load history.
An augmented Newton system solves this constraint with nodal equilibrium;
a numerical constitutive tangent differentiates the same damage and
projected-width update. The relative equilibrium tolerance is $10^{-6}$;
unconverged trials are rejected and their target intervals bisected without
committing damage.
% EXPERIMENTAL_METRICS_BEGIN
The initial 600 target intervals to 0.2~mm produce 608, 606 and 601 accepted
increments after eight, six and one rejected/bisected trials. Coarse,
medium and fine peaks are 16.64, 16.40 and 16.53~kN, versus the digitized
19.85~kN: errors are $-16.18\%$, $-17.39\%$ and $-16.76\%$.
The peak spread is 1.46\% of their mean. On 1,001 uniform points over
0--0.2~mm, experimental curve RMS errors are 9.27\%, 9.25\% and 9.09\%
of experimental peak; medium--fine RMS difference is 0.35\% of fine peak.
Both peak and late softening tail are underpredicted. All accepted residuals
meet $10^{-6}$; the maximum is $9.84\times10^{-7}$ and maximum gauge error
is $1.24\times10^{-15}$~mm. The small mesh spread does not remove the
experimental discrepancy or establish general mesh independence.
Idealized platens, constitutive assumptions, digitization and untested
increment convergence limit interpretation.

\subsection{Controlled mesh sensitivity and computational scaling}

A controlled 2D mesh family contains 14{,}313, 25{,}042, and
56{,}216 CPS3 elements, corresponding to 14{,}686, 25{,}534, and
56{,}954 DOFs. The notched-beam geometry and material properties are
unchanged. Additional partitions place the supports exactly at
$x=50$ and $300$~mm and define a fixed 6.25~mm loading strip at the
top centre; all nodes on that strip receive the same displacement.
The 50~mm-wide central zone containing the notch is refined across
the beam height. Its nominal seeds are 1.25, 0.9375, and 0.625~mm;
the outer seeds are five times larger. This concentrates elements near
the expected damage path while retaining a coarser outer region.
These boundary sets and each complete Abaqus connectivity are exported
directly to MATLAB; mesh-file hashes are checked after Abaqus rebuilds. This family is distinct from the single-mesh increment study. Both programs use one computational thread/CPU.

The main MATLAB runs impose $-0.1$~mm in 2{,}000 increments
($|\Delta u|=5\times10^{-5}$~mm). The regularized runs update the
Oliver projected width; the control runs hold the softening calibration
width at $h_{\rm ref}=1.25$~mm on every mesh. This control uses one
fixed stress--strain law and disables element-size compensation.
A second complete MATLAB family halves $|\Delta u|$ on all three
meshes for both calibrations, using 4{,}000 increments.
The Abaqus jobs use the same maximum displacement and increment cap,
with automatic cutbacks available; their accepted counts are reported.

Damage dissipation is calculated by adding the undamaged elastic energy
density times the damage increase and element volume over all increments.
External work is integrated from reaction and imposed displacement. Stored
energy uses the damaged elastic stiffness. Their balance is checked as a
numerical diagnostic. Comparisons use CMOD 0.10~mm, so they measure partial
structural dissipation rather than complete fracture energy.

Coarse, medium and fine load--CMOD curves are overlaid separately for

and 6.78\% with the fixed law. The corresponding dissipation spreads

as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:mesh-study}(g,h).
Halving the increment changes Oliver peaks by $-0.89$ to $-1.37\%$
and dissipation by $-0.99$ to $-1.53\%$; the corresponding fixed-law
ranges are $-0.96$ to $-2.20\%$ and $-1.27$ to $-3.74\%$.
These finite-increment checks quantify sensitivity; they do not establish
asymptotic convergence. At CMOD 0.10~mm, the absolute energy-balance discrepancy
is 0.42--1.00\% of external work in the main runs
and 0.21--0.48\% in the refined runs.
The fine Oliver peak post-damage relative residual decreases from
0.0052 to 0.0029.
The residual and energy discrepancy remain explicit limits of the
sequential update.

material or assembly work. Exact meshes, input decks, complete curves,
diagnostics and analysis scripts accompany the study.

\section{Impact}

\texttt{FRACMATH} makes implementation choices observable through source
code, numerical histories and diagnostic scripts. It supports research into
how projected crack-band width, irreversible damage and increment size affect
structural response. The fixed-law control separates element-size compensation
from the remaining numerical sensitivity. In the controlled 2D family,
regularization reduces partial-dissipation spread from 18.21\% to 6.86\%;
the smaller-increment family retains this reduction. Residual and energy
records let users judge the associated equilibrium error before extending
the method.

The 3D tension example shows how to distinguish mesh sensitivity from
experimental agreement: a 1.46\% peak spread coexists with 16--17\%
experimental underprediction. Its interpolated gauge control, rejected-trial
handling and digitized reference provide inspectable starting points for
studying boundary conditions and constitutive assumptions.

The timing results show which parts of the calculation take most time.
Ninety observations on matched meshes show that moving element operations
to a GPU does not improve this implementation on the tested workstation,
where CPU sparse factorization dominates. Component scopes and Abaqus
iteration counts allow subsequent implementations to be compared with
explicit accuracy and timing definitions.

For teaching and reuse, the entry script and array guide connect each input
to a computed state and plotted response. The verified UMAT provides a second
implementation for examining strain conventions and regularization. Users
can reproduce the figures, change a documented routine and inspect the
resulting response, energy and residual histories. Independent adoption,
download statistics and commercial use have not been established.

% =====================================================================
\subsection{Limitations}

The model is local, isotropic and quasistatic, with one damage scalar;
crack closure and anisotropic degradation are unrepresented. The reported
mesh families do not establish general mesh or orientation independence.
The 3D tension peaks remain 16.18--17.39\% below experiment; mixed-mode and
torsion fields remain qualitative. The 2D sequential update leaves a
post-damage equilibrium residual; coupled outer iteration is future work.
The hybrid GPU supports 2D T3 elements with CPU sparse factorization.
Three timing observations per configuration on one workstation provide
observed ranges. Different equilibrium algorithms and timer scopes prevent
an accuracy-matched cross-program speed ranking.

% =====================================================================
\section{Conclusions}

\texttt{FRACMATH} is an inspectable MATLAB example of local damage
with an Oliver projected crack-band width. The study presents constitutive checks, controlled 2D mesh and increment studies,
structural energy diagnostics, and timing scopes that separate sparse
factorization from assembly in MATLAB. A geometry-matched 3D pure-tension
comparison quantifies peak and curve errors without fitting material
parameters.
The hybrid GPU and SMP study reports 90 timing observations on matched meshes. The mixed-mode and torsion cases remain qualitative, and
direct timers record UMAT call duration with explicit clock diagnostics.
Native profiling identifies assembly self work; complete assembly wall
cost remains unallocated.
