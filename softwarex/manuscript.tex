\title{FRACMATH: A vectorized MATLAB framework for continuum damage
mechanics with crack band regularization}

\author[unm]{Jaykumar~Mavani}
\author[unm]{Madura~Pathirage\corref{cor1}}
\ead{mpathirage@unm.edu}
\cortext[cor1]{Corresponding author}
\address[unm]{Gerald May Department of Civil, Construction, and Environmental Engineering,
University of New Mexico, Albuquerque, NM, USA}

\begin{abstract}
\texttt{FRACMATH} is a vectorized MATLAB reference implementation of local
isotropic damage with direction-dependent crack-band regularization.
Its contribution is an inspectable workflow linking mesh import, projected
width, constitutive history, sparse assembly, and solution.
Material-point tests check strain mapping, irreversible damage, unloading,
and fracture-energy calibration. A controlled three-mesh bending study
compares regularized and fixed softening laws, measures partial structural
dissipation, and checks load-increment sensitivity. Single-CPU Abaqus jobs
provide response and diagnostic timing comparisons. A three-dimensional
pure-tension case is compared with a digitized experimental curve;
mixed-mode and torsion examples illustrate damage localization. Source code, response histories,
energy diagnostics, and plotting scripts accompany the manuscript;
algorithmic and validation limitations are stated.
\end{abstract}

\begin{keyword}
continuum damage mechanics \sep crack-band regularization \sep finite
element method \sep MATLAB \sep Abaqus UMAT \sep quasibrittle fracture
\end{keyword}

\section*{Motivation and significance}

\texttt{FRACMATH} is intended for researchers and students who need to
inspect the numerical path from a strain field to a damage field and a
global response. The constitutive law and crack-band principle are established
methods \citep{deVree,oliver1989,bazant_oh}. The useful software question is
whether the implementation makes its assumptions, update sequence,
regularization length, and numerical limitations visible enough to check and
modify. The package therefore includes element-level routines, a 2D bending
case, two 3D examples, plotting scripts, and an Abaqus UMAT for independent
comparison. The controlled study examines how element-size compensation
affects structural response and dissipation, and how increment size and
sparse linear algebra limit the numerical workflow.

\subsection*{Related software and scope}

General-purpose finite element systems such as Abaqus \citep{abaqus} and
OOFEM \citep{oofem} provide broader element and analysis capabilities.
CALFEM \citep{calfem} provides an accessible MATLAB finite element teaching
environment. \texttt{FRACMATH} offers a narrower, code-readable example of
direction-dependent local crack-band damage in 2D and 3D. It is designed
for teaching, replication, and testing method changes, not as a claim of
faster or more accurate performance than those systems.

\section*{Software description}

A scalar damage variable $\omega\in[0,1]$ scales the elastic stress:
$\boldsymbol\sigma=(1-\omega)\mathbb C_0:\boldsymbol\varepsilon$.
The history variable $\kappa_{n+1}=\max(\kappa_n,\tilde\varepsilon_{n+1})$
makes the update irreversible. The modified von Mises measure is
\begin{equation}
\tilde\varepsilon = \frac{k-1}{2k(1-2\nu)}I_1
  + \frac{1}{2k}\sqrt{\left(\frac{k-1}{1-2\nu}\right)^2I_1^2
  + \frac{12k}{(1+\nu)^2}J_2},\quad
J_2=\frac{(e_1-e_2)^2+(e_2-e_3)^2+(e_3-e_1)^2}{6},
\label{eq:equiv}
\end{equation}
where $k=f_c/f_t$. For the 2D plane-stress element,
$e_3=-\nu(e_1+e_2)/(1-\nu)$. Both the MATLAB and UMAT implementations use Eq.~\eqref{eq:equiv}.

The damage law is
\begin{equation}
\omega(\kappa)=1-\frac{\kappa_0}{\kappa}
\exp\!\left[-\frac{\kappa-\kappa_0}{\varepsilon_f-\kappa_0}\right],
\quad \kappa_0=f_t/E,\quad
\varepsilon_f=\frac{\kappa_0}{2}+\frac{G_F}{h f_t}.
\label{eq:softening}
\end{equation}
The Oliver projected width for a three-node triangle is
\begin{equation}
h(\mathbf n)=\frac{2}{\sum_{a=1}^3|\nabla N_a\cdot\mathbf n|},
\label{eq:oliver-t3}
\end{equation}
with $\mathbf n$ along maximum principal strain. The 3D tetrahedral
implementation uses the analogous projected width. These are local
regularization formulas; the material-point energy check below does not
by itself prove structural mesh objectivity.

\subsection*{Numerical workflow and code map}

The 2D driver \texttt{solver\_main\_3pb.m} reads the mesh and support/loading
sets, precomputes elastic element matrices and shape-function gradients,
and advances prescribed displacement. At each increment it (1) assembles
the secant stiffness from the previous damage field, (2) factors its free
block with \texttt{decomposition}, (3) solves equilibrium for the frozen
damage state, (4) obtains element strains with \texttt{pagemtimes}, updates
$h$, $\kappa$, and $\omega$ in \texttt{damage\_update}, and (5) reassembles
the damaged stiffness for reaction forces and the next increment. The
factorization helper first attempts Cholesky and falls back to LU if
that attempt fails. It is reused only within a displacement increment. There is no
outer equilibrium iteration after the damage update, so this is a
sequential secant scheme rather than a fully converged Newton solve.
The post-update free-DOF residual is exported for each increment.

The optional Abaqus path uses \texttt{run\_3pb\_abaqus\_OLIVER\_T3\_FAST.py}
to write a table of T3 shape-function gradients. The Fortran
\texttt{cdm\_umat\_2d\_OLIVER\_T3\_FAST.for} reads that table to compute
Eq.~\eqref{eq:oliver-t3}; it does not use \texttt{CELENT} when the table
is present. The UMAT supplies the degraded elastic secant matrix as
\texttt{DDSDDE}, rather than a consistent derivative of the evolving
damage law. Its approximate tangent requires reporting equilibrium
iteration counts with timings. Table initialization currently requires
one Abaqus analysis thread.

\subsection*{Material-point verification}

A one-element uniaxial test checks $\tilde\varepsilon=\varepsilon$ in
tension and $\tilde\varepsilon=|\varepsilon|/k$ in compression, monotone
damage under unload/reload, and $h\int\sigma\,d\varepsilon\approx G_F$
for four prescribed projected widths. Numerical integration to a large
terminal strain gives the following results for $G_F=0.090$~N/mm.
A standalone driver linked to the unchanged Fortran UMAT repeats the
strain-mapping, projected-width, energy, and unload/reload assertions;
its energy results agree with the MATLAB values at the precision shown.
Sixteen additional UMAT checks combine these four triangle widths with
loading directions of $15^\circ$, $45^\circ$, $75^\circ$, and $90^\circ$.
Projected widths agree with Eq.~\eqref{eq:oliver-t3}, and the largest
absolute relative energy error is $1.78\times10^{-6}$. These are local
implementation checks, not evidence of structural orientation independence.

\subsection*{Benchmark 1: Notched 2D three-point bending}

The Gr\'egoire beam \citep{gregoire2013} has depth $D=100$~mm,
support span $S=250$~mm, overall length 350~mm, thickness 50~mm, and
a midspan notch of depth 20~mm and width 2.5~mm. Concrete parameters
are $E=37{,}000$~MPa, $\nu=0.20$, $f_t=3.5$~MPa, $f_c=35$~MPa, and
$G_F=0.090$~N/mm. The 14{,}268 CPS3-element mesh has 7{,}319 nodes.
The left support is pinned, the right is a roller, and a prescribed
vertical displacement reaches $-0.2$~mm. CMOD is the mean horizontal
displacement difference across the notch mouth. Figure~\ref{fig:b1}
uses the mesh drawing: the 250~mm dimension denotes support
span, while 350~mm is the overall length.

The comparison in Fig.~\ref{fig:b1} uses the 10{,}000-step

4.26~kN (CMOD 0.02835~mm, step 174), while the 10{,}000-step
run peaks at 4.03~kN (CMOD 0.02579~mm, step 1612). This
{5.9}\% change in peak load shows that increment
size matters for the sequential scheme; these results are numerical
examples, not an increment-converged validation against experiment.
The normalized post-update free-DOF residual at the peak is
0.016 for 1{,}000 steps and 0.002 for
10{,}000 steps. A single-thread Abaqus/Standard 2024 UMAT run produced a peak of 4.00~kN at CMOD 0.02540~mm. The 10{,}000-step MATLAB peak differs by 0.68\% in load and 1.51\% in peak CMOD. The close response is a code-to-code check, not experimental validation.

The completed Abaqus \texttt{.msg} reports 1{,}136
accepted increments, 4{,}817 equilibrium iterations/solver passes, 4{,}799
matrix decompositions, one cutback, and 45 acceptances under the
alternate force tolerance. Its summed per-pass sparse-solver elapsed time
is 100.2~s out of 832~s reported wall clock.
The remaining wall time includes element/UMAT operations, assembly,
convergence and output, which the \texttt{.msg} does not separate.
Thus no attribution of the remainder to the UMAT or speed ranking
between codes is supported. The MATLAB run used 10{,}000 fixed
increments, while Abaqus used automatic increments, so the two
wall-clock totals are not a controlled benchmark.
The repository now includes a parser that reproduces these counts and
timings from the archived \texttt{.msg} and MATLAB timing file. New MATLAB
runs export per-step component timings, iteration counts, and the
post-damage equilibrium residual. The implementation walkthrough maps
these measurements to the corresponding solver functions. The preserved
10{,}000-step run predates this per-step export; its aggregate timer and
response data are retained without alteration.

\subsection*{Benchmark 2: 3D Nooru-Mohamed Tests}
% =====================================================================

The Nooru-Mohamed specimen is a square double edge notched concrete
panel, $200 \times 200 \times 50$~mm, with two horizontal notches 25~mm
long cut from opposite edges at mid height \citep{nooru1992}; the
central ligament between the notch tips experiences a mixed
mode stress state. The illustrative proportional-displacement case prescribes a vertical (mode I)
displacement on the top edge and a proportional horizontal
(shear) displacement on the side edges, with a shear to tension ratio
$\gamma = 0.6$ and the bottom edge held fixed, ramped together under
displacement control over 900 increments to a top displacement of
0.5~mm. The material is concrete with $E = 29{,}000$~MPa, $\nu = 0.20$,
$f_t = 3.0$~MPa, $G_F = 0.110$~N/mm, and $k = f_c/f_t = 10$ (so
$f_c = 30$~MPa).

The proportional-loading damage panels illustrate the workflow. A separate
pure-tension comparison uses specimen 47-05 with two glued platens
(Figure 3.16, printed page 45 of \citep{nooru1992}). Its load--displacement
curve is manually digitized; estimated reading uncertainty is
$\pm 0.00102$~mm and $\pm 164$~N. The nominal mesh has 3,933 nodes and
18,927 TET4 elements. Existing material parameters above are retained
without fitting. Bottom vertical displacements are fixed, the top platen
has a common unknown vertical displacement, and minimal lateral pins
remove rigid modes. The mean displacement of four interpolated 65~mm
vertical gauges at $x=30/170$~mm on both faces controls the load history.
An augmented Newton system solves this constraint with nodal equilibrium;
a numerical constitutive tangent differentiates the same damage and
projected-width update. The relative equilibrium tolerance is $10^{-6}$;
unconverged increments are rejected.
% EXPERIMENTAL_METRICS_BEGIN
With 600 increments to a mean gauge displacement of 0.2~mm, the peak
is 18.18~kN at 0.0090~mm, versus digitized estimates of 19.85~kN at
0.00659~mm: the peak-load error is $-8.45\%$. The RMS curve error on
1,001 uniformly spaced points over 0--0.2~mm is 8.11\% of experimental
peak load. The late softening tail is underpredicted. All accepted
increments meet the equilibrium tolerance, with a maximum relative
residual of $9.93\times10^{-7}$. A 1,200-increment attempt fails equilibrium
at increment 55 (residual $4.19\times10^{-3}$); a 300-increment attempt
also fails at increment 14 (residual $1.43\times10^{-2}$). Both are excluded. Thus this
single nominal-mesh comparison provides quantitative experimental
error measures, but does not establish increment convergence or general
3D validation. Experimental notch scatter, idealized platens, and
curve-digitization uncertainty also limit the comparison.

% =====================================================================

The torsion illustration uses a $400\times100\times100$~mm beam.
The published Brokenshire test uses steel collars with three supported
arms and one loaded arm \citep{brokenshire1996,jefferson_torsion,jefferson2005}.
Its measured response is applied load versus clip-gauge CMOD.
The rotation-controlled model illustrated here uses different end constraints;
a load--CMOD validation therefore requires a model of the collar and gauge
configuration. The projected width follows the local principal direction,
producing a nonplanar damage path whose mesh convergence is unverified.

In the model one end face is fully fixed while the opposite end face is
given a prescribed rigid-body twist $\theta$ about the beam axis under
rotation control, ramped to
$\theta_{\mathrm{end}} = 3.0\times10^{-3}$~rad over 140 increments; the
reaction torque is recovered from the twisted end.

The material is plain concrete with $E = 35{,}000$~MPa, $\nu = 0.20$,
$f_t = 3.0$~MPa, $G_F = 0.080$~N/mm, $\kappa_0=f_t/E=8.57\times10^{-5}$, and
$k = f_c/f_t = 10$ (so $f_c = 30$~MPa), with the same exponential
softening scaled element by element through the crack band length.

The TET4 mesh carries a refined layer around the notch front. The damage
update is under-relaxed with a factor of 0.35 to keep the staggered
fixed point iteration stable through the steep post-peak branch, and
each linear solve reuses a single AMD ordering with a Cholesky
factorization.

This case is a qualitative demonstration of direction-dependent
crack-band regularization under nonplanar, torsion-driven crack growth:
the predicted damage band nucleates at the notch front and spirals
toward the loaded corner (Figure~\ref{fig:b3-overview}),
qualitatively similar to the published fracture description
\citep{brokenshire1996}. The available torsion history does not provide a valid experimental
CMOD channel; it supports the qualitative damage illustration only.

\subsection*{Controlled mesh sensitivity and computational scaling}

A controlled 2D mesh family contains 14{,}313, 25{,}042, and
56{,}216 CPS3 elements, corresponding to 14{,}686, 25{,}534, and
56{,}954 DOFs. The notched-beam geometry and material properties are
unchanged. Additional partitions place the supports exactly at
$x=50$ and $300$~mm and define a fixed 6.25~mm loading strip at the
top centre; all nodes on that strip receive the same displacement.
The 50~mm-wide central zone containing the notch is refined across
the beam height. Its nominal seeds are 1.25, 0.9375, and 0.625~mm;
the outer seeds are five times larger. This concentrates elements near
the expected damage path while retaining a coarser outer region.
In all three main Oliver runs, elements with $\omega\geq0.95$ at
peak load have centroids inside this refined region, at heights
20.2--37.6~mm above the beam bottom. This checks the chosen refinement
location, without asserting convergence of the damage-band geometry.
These boundary sets and each complete Abaqus connectivity are exported
directly to MATLAB; mesh-file hashes are checked after Abaqus rebuilds. This family is distinct from the single-mesh increment study. Both programs use one computational thread/CPU.

The main MATLAB runs impose $-0.1$~mm in 2{,}000 increments
($|\Delta u|=5\times10^{-5}$~mm). The regularized runs update the
Oliver projected width; the control runs hold the softening calibration
width at $h_{\rm ref}=1.25$~mm on every mesh. This control uses one
fixed stress--strain law and disables element-size compensation.
A second complete MATLAB family halves $|\Delta u|$ on all three
meshes for both calibrations, using 4{,}000 increments.
The Abaqus jobs use the same maximum displacement and increment cap,
with automatic cutbacks available; their accepted counts are reported.

For each MATLAB increment, the undamaged energy density is
$Y_e=\tfrac12\boldsymbol\varepsilon_e^{\mathsf T}
\mathbf D\boldsymbol\varepsilon_e$. Accumulated damage dissipation is
evaluated by the positive end-of-step quadrature
\begin{equation}
\mathcal D_N=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_e
 Y_e^n\bigl(\omega_e^n-\omega_e^{n-1}\bigr)V_e.
\label{eq:study-dissipation}
\end{equation}
External work uses trapezoidal integration of reaction versus prescribed
displacement; stored energy is $\sum_e(1-\omega_e)Y_eV_e$.
The discrepancy between external work and stored energy plus
$\mathcal D_N$ is retained as a numerical diagnostic.
Energy comparisons use a common CMOD of 0.10~mm and represent partial
structural dissipation, not a measurement of complete fracture energy.

% MESH_STUDY_RESULTS_BEGIN
Figure~\ref{fig:mesh-study} compares the two softening calibrations.
Across the three main runs, peak-load spread, defined as
$(\max-\min)/\mathrm{mean}$, is 6.19\% with Oliver regularization
and 6.78\% with the fixed law. The corresponding dissipation spreads

\section*{Impact}

The main value of \texttt{FRACMATH} is that users can trace a complete
damage calculation through mesh import, element matrices, projected
bandwidth, constitutive history, sparse solve, and plotted response.
The material-point test and the timing scopes are small,
inspectable checks that expose two issues in the prior version: an
incorrect 2D invariant coefficient and mislabelled factorization time.
The 2D and 3D examples provide starting cases for method development
and classroom use. The supplied scripts permit the figures to be
regenerated from the associated data.

% =====================================================================
\subsection*{Limitations}

The model is local, isotropic, quasistatic, and intended for monotonic
loading. A single damage scalar does not represent crack closure or
anisotropic degradation. The material-point tests verify softening calibration. The controlled
2D family tests mesh sensitivity for one geometry and mesh construction;
it does not establish general mesh or orientation independence.
The 3D pure-tension comparison uses one nominal mesh and idealized platens;
3D mesh independence remains untested. Mixed-mode and torsion images
remain qualitative. The 2D
sequential update leaves a nonzero post-damage equilibrium residual;
the archived increment study and new refinement checks quantify its
effect. A fully coupled outer equilibrium iteration is future work. The current Abaqus UMAT
gradient-table initialization is not safe for multithreaded analysis.
The larger study reports completed-job diagnostics, but the algorithms and
timing scopes differ. An accuracy-matched speed ranking and multithreaded
scaling remain unverified; no GPU performance claim is made.

% =====================================================================
\section*{Conclusions}

\texttt{FRACMATH} is an inspectable MATLAB example of local damage
with an Oliver projected crack-band width. The study presents constitutive checks, controlled 2D mesh and increment studies,
structural energy diagnostics, and timing scopes that separate sparse
factorization from assembly in MATLAB. A nominal-mesh 3D pure-tension
comparison provides experimental error measures without fitting material
parameters. The mixed-mode and torsion cases remain qualitative, and
separate Abaqus material/assembly costs remain unmeasured.
